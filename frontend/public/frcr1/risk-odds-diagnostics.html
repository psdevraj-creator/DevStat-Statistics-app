Risk, Odds & Diagnostic Tests

Absolute and relative risk, odds ratio, NNT, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, likelihood ratios, ROC curves, and AUC — GCSE to FRCR level.

Risk — The Basic Idea

Risk = number of people with the outcome / total number of people at risk.

Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR) = Risk in control group − Risk in treatment group. If 20% of untreated patients have an event vs 12% of treated patients, ARR = 20% − 12% = 8%.

Relative Risk (RR) = Risk in treatment group / Risk in control group. RR = 12%/20% = 0.60. The treatment reduces risk to 60% of the control risk (a 40% relative reduction).

Number Needed to Treat (NNT) = 1 / ARR. NNT = 1/0.08 = 12.5 ≈ 13. You need to treat 13 patients to prevent one bad outcome.

⚠️ Relative vs Absolute Risk
A treatment may show a "50% relative risk reduction" but this could mean reducing risk from 2% to 1% (ARR = 1%, NNT = 100) or from 50% to 25% (ARR = 25%, NNT = 4). Always look at absolute risk, not just relative risk!

Diagnostic Test Performance

2×2 table: Test result (positive/negative) vs gold standard (disease present/absent).

  • Sensitivity = P(test + | disease +) = true positives / all diseased. "How good is the test at detecting disease?"
  • Specificity = P(test − | disease −) = true negatives / all non-diseased. "How good is the test at ruling out disease?"
  • PPV = P(disease + | test +) = true positives / all positive tests. Depends on disease prevalence.
  • NPV = P(disease − | test −) = true negatives / all negative tests. Depends on disease prevalence.

Figure 1: The 2×2 diagnostic test table. TP/TN (green) are correct results; FP/FN (red) are errors. Sensitivity and specificity are prevalence-independent test properties. PPV and NPV depend on disease prevalence.

Relative Risk & Odds Ratio — Complete Guide

For a 2×2 table comparing two groups:

Event No Event Total
Treatmentaba+b
Controlcdc+d

Risk in treatment group = a/(a+b). Risk in control group = c/(c+d).

Relative Risk (RR) = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)]. RR < 1 means treatment reduces risk.

Odds Ratio (OR) = (a/b) / (c/d) = ad / bc. OR < 1 means treatment reduces odds.

Relative Risk Reduction (RRR) = 1 − RR. Often expressed as percentage. "Treatment reduces risk by X% relative to control."

Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR) = c/(c+d) − a/(a+b) = risk difference.

Number Needed to Treat (NNT) = 1 / ARR. Always round up. NNT = 1/0.08 = 12.5 → 13.

Number Needed to Harm (NNH) = 1 / (risk of adverse event in treatment group − risk in control group).

⚠️ Exam Trap: OR vs RR
When the outcome is COMMON (>10%), OR > RR. Example: event rate 40% in controls and 20% in treatment. RR = 0.20/0.40 = 0.50. OR = (0.2/0.8)/(0.4/0.6) = 0.25/0.67 = 0.375. The OR (0.375) substantially underestimates the effect compared to RR (0.50) when expressed as a "reduction." For rare outcomes (<10%), OR ≈ RR numerically.

Diagnostic Test Evaluation — Full Detail

Sensitivity = a / (a + c). A highly sensitive test rules OUT disease when negative (SnOUT). High sensitivity → few false negatives.

Specificity = d / (b + d). A highly specific test rules IN disease when positive (SpIN). High specificity → few false positives.

Positive Predictive Value (PPV) = a / (a + b). PPV depends on disease prevalence. PPV = (sensitivity × prevalence) / (sensitivity × prevalence + (1−specificity) × (1−prevalence)).

Negative Predictive Value (NPV) = d / (c + d). NPV also depends on prevalence.

Key point: PPV and NPV are prevalence-dependent. A test with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity will have PPV = 95% if prevalence = 50%, but only PPV = 66% if prevalence = 20%. Always consider the disease prevalence in your population when interpreting PPV/NPV.

Likelihood Ratios

Likelihood ratios are prevalence-independent measures of test performance — preferred over PPV/NPV for clinical decision-making.

Positive Likelihood Ratio (LR+) = sensitivity / (1 − specificity). How much more likely is a positive test in diseased vs non-diseased patients? LR+ > 10 is strong evidence to rule in disease.

Negative Likelihood Ratio (LR−) = (1 − sensitivity) / specificity. How much less likely is a negative test in diseased vs non-diseased patients? LR− < 0.1 is strong evidence to rule out disease.

Using likelihood ratios with pre-test probability:

  1. Convert pre-test probability (prevalence) to pre-test odds: odds = p/(1−p).
  2. Post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR.
  3. Convert back to post-test probability: p = odds/(1+odds).

Fagan's nomogram: A graphical tool to estimate post-test probability given pre-test probability and LR. No calculation needed — aligns three points on a scale.

ROC Curves & AUC

ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve: Plots sensitivity (true positive rate) vs 1−specificity (false positive rate) across all possible threshold values of a continuous test. Each point on the curve represents a different cut-off.

AUC (Area Under the Curve):

  • AUC = 1.0: perfect test (100% sensitivity, 100% specificity at some threshold).
  • AUC > 0.9: excellent diagnostic accuracy.
  • AUC 0.8–0.9: good diagnostic accuracy.
  • AUC 0.7–0.8: fair diagnostic accuracy.
  • AUC = 0.5: no better than chance (diagonal line).

Choosing the optimal threshold: The point on the ROC curve closest to the top-left corner (0,1) maximises sensitivity + specificity. Alternatively, choose the threshold that maximises the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1).

Comparing tests: AUC can be used to compare two diagnostic tests — larger AUC = better discrimination. Tests with partial AUC overlap may have different optimal uses (one may be better at ruling in, another at ruling out).

Agreement Between Tests

Cohen's Kappa (κ): Measures agreement between two raters or two tests beyond chance.

κ = (observed agreement − expected agreement) / (1 − expected agreement).

Interpretation:

  • κ < 0: poor agreement (worse than chance).
  • κ 0.0–0.2: slight agreement.
  • κ 0.21–0.40: fair agreement.
  • κ 0.41–0.60: moderate agreement.
  • κ 0.61–0.80: substantial agreement.
  • κ 0.81–1.0: almost perfect agreement.

Weighted Kappa: For ordinal outcomes, accounts for degree of disagreement (a difference of 1 category is less serious than a difference of 3 categories).

Bland-Altman plot: For continuous measurements, plots the difference between two measurements against their mean. Shows bias (mean difference) and limits of agreement (±1.96 × SD of differences). Used for method comparison studies.

Essential Exam Facts

RR = risk_treated / risk_control. RR < 1 = protective effect.
OR = (a/b)/(c/d) = ad/bc. OR ≈ RR only for rare outcomes (<10%).
ARR = risk_control − risk_treated. NNT = 1/ARR (round up).
RRR = 1 − RR. Misleading when baseline risk is low.
Sensitivity = TP/all diseased. SnOUT: high sensitivity → negative test rules OUT disease.
Specificity = TN/all non-diseased. SpIN: high specificity → positive test rules IN disease.
PPV and NPV are PREVALENCE-DEPENDENT. LR+ and LR− are not.
LR+ = sens/(1−spec). >10 = strong rule-in evidence.
LR− = (1−sens)/spec. <0.1 = strong rule-out evidence.
AUC: >0.9 excellent, 0.8–0.9 good, 0.7–0.8 fair, 0.5 = chance.
Cohen's Kappa: agreement beyond chance. 0.61+ = substantial.
⚠️ Common Exam Traps
• "50% relative risk reduction" from 2% to 1% gives ARR = 1%, NNT = 100 — not impressive!
• PPV decreases as prevalence decreases — a positive test in a low-prevalence population is more likely to be false positive.
• AUC = 0.5 means the test is no better than flipping a coin — not "no discrimination at all."
• Kappa can be low even with high observed agreement if the expected agreement is also high (prevalence bias).
• OR is NOT the same as RR — do not interpret OR as "X times more likely" unless the outcome is rare.
🏥 Why This Matters Clinically

Interpreting trial results with patients: When discussing treatment benefits with patients, NNT is the most clinically intuitive measure. "You would need to treat 13 patients like you to prevent one heart attack" is more meaningful than "the hazard ratio was 0.75."

Screening programmes: PPV is critical in screening — even with a highly sensitive/specific test, PPV may be low in a low-prevalence screening population. This explains why mass screening for rare diseases produces many false positives.

Choosing diagnostic tests: A high-sensitivity test (LR− low) is best for ruling out disease (screening). A high-specificity test (LR+ high) is best for confirming disease after a positive screening result.

Risk communication: Relative risk reductions are commonly reported in the media and even in medical journals because they sound more impressive. Always convert to ARR and NNT to understand the actual clinical impact. The FRCR exam frequently tests this distinction.

Critical appraisal: When reading a diagnostic test study: (1) Was the test compared to an appropriate gold standard? (2) Was the test evaluated in the relevant clinical population (not a case-control sample that inflates accuracy)? (3) Are PPV/NPV reported with prevalence? (4) Is the ROC curve shown with AUC? (5) Are LRs reported for different thresholds?

📐 Worked Examples

Scenario: A trial of a new drug reports: mortality 8% in drug group vs 20% in placebo group. Calculate RR, RRR, ARR and NNT.

Solution:

RR = 0.08 / 0.20 = 0.40. The drug reduces risk to 40% of placebo (60% relative reduction).

RRR = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60 = 60%. This sounds impressive.

ARR = 0.20 − 0.08 = 0.12 = 12%. The absolute benefit is 12 percentage points.

NNT = 1 / 0.12 = 8.3 → 9. You need to treat 9 patients with the drug to prevent one death.

Clinical interpretation: The 60% relative risk reduction sounds dramatic, but the ARR tells you the absolute benefit is modest — 12 out of 100 patients benefit. The NNT of 9 is reasonably good (many effective cancer treatments have NNT > 20).

Scenario: A new CT screening test for lung cancer has 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity. Prevalence of lung cancer in the screening population is 2%. Calculate PPV and NPV.

Solution: Assume 10,000 people screened. Disease present: 200 (2%). Test positive: 180 TP (0.9 × 200) + 490 FP (0.05 × 9,800) = 670 positive tests. PPV = 180/670 = 26.9%. Only 27% of positive tests are true positives!

NPV = 9,310 / 9,330 = 99.8% (9,800−490 = 9,310 true negatives; 200−180 = 20 false negatives). A negative result is highly reassuring.

Key insight: Even with good sensitivity and specificity, PPV in screening is low due to low prevalence. This is why screening tests need very high specificity (>99%) to avoid overwhelming false positives.

Scenario: A patient has a pre-test probability of pulmonary embolism of 15%. A D-dimer test has LR+ = 2.5 and LR− = 0.1. Calculate the post-test probability after a positive and negative result.

Solution: Pre-test odds = 0.15/(1−0.15) = 0.176.

After positive test: post-test odds = 0.176 × 2.5 = 0.44. Post-test probability = 0.44/(1+0.44) = 0.31 = 31%.

After negative test: post-test odds = 0.176 × 0.1 = 0.0176. Post-test probability = 0.0176/1.0176 = 1.7%.

Clinical interpretation: A positive D-dimer only raises PE probability from 15% to 31% — not enough to confirm PE. A negative D-dimer lowers probability to 1.7% — very good at ruling out PE (this is why D-dimer is used as a rule-out test in suspected PE).

🧠 Active Recall Questions

  • Q: What is the difference between relative risk reduction and absolute risk reduction?
    A: RRR = (risk_control − risk_treated)/risk_control. ARR = risk_control − risk_treated. RRR is independent of baseline risk; ARR depends on baseline risk. Always check ARR to understand clinical impact.
  • Q: Why do PPV and NPV depend on disease prevalence?
    A: PPV = TP/(TP+FP). As prevalence decreases, FP increases (because more non-diseased people are tested), so PPV decreases. NPV = TN/(TN+FN). As prevalence decreases, TN increases, so NPV increases.
  • Q: What are the advantages of likelihood ratios over PPV/NPV?
    A: LR+ and LR− are prevalence-independent (intrinsic properties of the test). They allow calculation of post-test probability from pre-test probability, which can be tailored to individual patients.
  • Q: What does AUC measure and what is the interpretation?
    A: AUC = probability that a randomly selected diseased patient has a higher test value than a randomly selected non-diseased patient. >0.9 excellent, 0.8–0.9 good, 0.7–0.8 fair, 0.5 = chance.
  • Q: What does Cohen's Kappa measure?
    A: Agreement between two raters/tests beyond chance. κ = (observed − expected) / (1 − expected). 0.61–0.80 = substantial agreement.

1/ARR. Number of patients who must be treated to prevent one event.

ad/bc from 2×2 table. ≠ RR when outcome is common.

(1−Sensitivity) / Specificity. <0.1 = strong rule-out.
