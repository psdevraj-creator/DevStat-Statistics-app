Normal Distribution & Central Limit Theorem

The normal distribution, z-scores, standard error, and the central limit theorem — the foundation of parametric statistics.

The Normal Distribution

Many biological measurements follow a bell-shaped curve called the normal (Gaussian) distribution. Key features:

  • Symmetric about the mean.
  • Mean = median = mode (all at the centre).
  • 68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean.
  • 95% of values lie within ±1.96 SD (~2 SD) of the mean.
  • 99.7% of values lie within ±3 SD of the mean.

Standard Normal Distribution: A normal distribution with mean = 0 and SD = 1. We convert any normal distribution to this using the z-score: z = (x − μ) / σ. The z-score tells you how many SDs a value is from the mean.

Figure 1: The normal distribution showing the percentage of values within 1, 1.96, and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule)

For any normally distributed dataset:

  • ~68% of data falls within 1 SD of the mean.
  • ~95% of data falls within 2 SD (actually 1.96) of the mean.
  • ~99.7% of data falls within 3 SD of the mean.

This rule allows you to quickly estimate whether a value is unusual. For example, if mean height = 170 cm and SD = 10 cm, then a person 200 cm tall (3 SD above the mean) is in the top 0.15% of the population.

Normal (Gaussian) Distribution — Formal Definition

The normal distribution is defined by two parameters: N(μ, σ²). The probability density function is:

f(x) = (1 / σ√(2π)) × exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))

Properties:

  • Domain: −∞ to +∞ (theoretical — but practical values lie within ±3 SD).
  • Total area under the curve = 1.
  • Inflection points at μ ± σ.
  • Median, mode, and mean are equal.
  • The distribution is fully described by μ and σ² — no third or fourth parameters needed.

Standard Normal (Z) distribution: N(0, 1). Convert using z = (x − μ) / σ.

Key z-values to memorise for FRCR:

  • z = 1.645 → 90% of values (two-tailed). One-tailed 95%.
  • z = 1.96 → 95% of values (two-tailed). This is the most important z-value for 95% CIs.
  • z = 2.58 → 99% of values (two-tailed).
  • z = 3.29 → 99.9% of values (two-tailed).

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The Central Limit Theorem is arguably the most important theorem in statistics for medical research. It states:

The sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as sample size (n) increases, regardless of the underlying population distribution.

What this means in practice:

  • Even if the original data is skewed (e.g., survival times, PSA levels), the mean of many samples will be approximately normally distributed.
  • This justifies using parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) for n ≥ 30 even when the data is non-normal.
  • The approximation improves as n increases. For n = 30, the approximation is usually adequate. For n = 100, it is very good.
  • For n < 30, the data itself should be approximately normal for parametric tests to be valid.

Mathematical form: If X₁, X₂, ..., Xₙ are independent random variables from a distribution with mean μ and variance σ², then:

X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n) as n → ∞

This means: the mean of the sample means = μ (unbiased). The variance of the sample means = σ²/n (smaller for larger samples).

Figure 2: The Central Limit Theorem. Even if the original population is skewed, the distribution of sample means (the sampling distribution) approaches a normal distribution as n increases. The spread of this normal distribution is the Standard Error of the Mean (SEM = σ/√n).

Standard Error of the Mean (SEM)

SEM = σ / √n (population) or s / √n (sample estimate).

The SEM measures how precisely the sample mean estimates the population mean. It is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the mean.

Key relationships:

  • SEM is always smaller than SD (because √n > 1 for n > 1).
  • SEM decreases as n increases — larger samples give more precise estimates.
  • SEM decreases as variability (σ) decreases — more homogeneous populations give more precise estimates.
  • To halve the SEM, you need to quadruple the sample size (because SEM = σ/√n).
⚠️ Exam Trap: SD vs SEM
SD describes spread of individual observations (used for descriptive statistics of the sample). SEM describes precision of the mean estimate (used for confidence intervals). They are NOT interchangeable. In the FRCR exam, if a question asks for "variability of individual measurements," the answer is SD, not SEM.

Figure 3: SD describes spread of individual observations (wide bell). SEM = SD/√n describes how precisely the sample mean estimates the population mean (narrower bell, shrinks with larger n).

Checking Normality — How to Assess

Before using parametric tests, you need to check whether the data is approximately normally distributed. Methods include:

Visual methods:

  • Histogram: Look for symmetric bell shape. Skew or multiple peaks suggest non-normality.
  • Q-Q plot (Quantile-Quantile plot): Plot sample quantiles against theoretical normal quantiles. Points should lie approximately on the diagonal line. Systematic deviations (curvature, S-shape) indicate non-normality.
  • Box plot: Look for symmetry around the median and presence of outliers.

Statistical tests (formal but may be oversensitive with large n):

  • Shapiro-Wilk test: Best for n < 50. Tests H₀: data is normally distributed. Small p-value → reject normality.
  • Kolmogorov-Smirnov test: Can be used for larger samples but less powerful than Shapiro-Wilk.
  • D'Agostino-Pearson test: Tests both skewness and kurtosis simultaneously.

Practical rule: For FRCR purposes, histograms + Q-Q plots are usually sufficient. Formal tests are overly sensitive for large n (may detect trivial deviations) and underpowered for small n (fail to detect important deviations).

Transformations for Non-Normal Data

When data is non-normal, you have options:

  • Log transformation: y = ln(x). Used for right-skewed data (survival times, biomarker levels). After transformation, data may become approximately normal.
  • Square root transformation: y = √x. Used for count data (number of metastases).
  • Box-Cox transformation: A family of power transformations (y = x^λ − 1 / λ for λ ≠ 0, y = ln(x) for λ = 0). The optimal λ is estimated from the data.
  • Non-parametric tests: If transformation fails, use the non-parametric alternative (Mann-Whitney instead of t-test, Spearman instead of Pearson).

Important: When you log-transform data for analysis, report results on the original scale by exponentiating back (geometric mean, ratio of geometric means). The geometric mean is always ≤ the arithmetic mean for positively skewed data.

Essential Exam Facts

Normal distribution: N(μ, σ²). Symmetric, bell-shaped.
68% within ±1σ, 95% within ±1.96σ, 99.7% within ±3σ of mean.
z = (x − μ)/σ. z = 1.96 gives two-tailed 95% range.
CLT: sample mean ~ N(μ, σ²/n) as n → ∞, regardless of underlying distribution.
SEM = σ/√n. SEM < SD. SEM decreases as n increases.
To halve SEM, quadruple the sample size.
SD = spread of observations. SEM = precision of mean estimate.
CLT justifies parametric tests for n ≥ 30 even with non-normal data.
Log transformation: for right-skewed data. Geometric mean after back-transform.
Shapiro-Wilk: formal test of normality. Q-Q plot: visual check.
⚠️ Common Exam Traps
• SEM is NOT the standard deviation of the sample — it is the SD of the sampling distribution of the mean.
• The CLT applies to the SAMPLE MEAN, not to the original data. The original data can be highly non-normal.
• z = 1.96 gives the 95% RANGE (two-tailed). For a one-tailed 95% CI, z = 1.645.
• The normal distribution is theoretical — real data is never perfectly normal, only approximately so.
• For n < 30, the CLT provides weak protection — check normality of the original data before using parametric tests.
🏥 Why This Matters Clinically

Reference ranges: Clinical reference ranges (e.g., normal creatinine 60–110 μmol/L) are typically calculated as mean ± 1.96 SD from a healthy population. This assumes the biomarker is normally distributed. If it is skewed (like PSA), reference ranges use percentiles directly.

Understanding SEM in trial reports: When a trial reports "mean survival improved by 3.2 months (SEM 1.1)," the SEM tells you about precision. A smaller SEM (from a larger sample or less variable endpoint) gives more confidence in the estimate. Always check whether the paper reports SD or SEM — they convey different information.

Sample size and precision: The relationship SEM = σ/√n explains why underpowered studies produce imprecise estimates. A study with n = 25 has SEM twice as large as a study with n = 100 — meaning the CI will be twice as wide and the result less informative.

Log transformation of biomarkers: Many clinical biomarkers (PSA, CRP, viral load) are log-normally distributed. When analysing these, researchers should log-transform before using parametric tests. Reporting the geometric mean is more clinically meaningful than the arithmetic mean for such data.

Critically appraising normality claims: When reading a paper, look for: (1) Did they check normality? (2) What method did they use (visual or formal)? (3) For small n (<30), did they verify the normality assumption or use a non-parametric alternative? Many published papers use parametric tests without checking assumptions — this can lead to invalid conclusions.

📐 Worked Examples

Scenario: Mean creatinine in a healthy population = 85 μmol/L, SD = 15 μmol/L. A patient has creatinine = 120 μmol/L. What is the z-score? What percentage of healthy people have creatinine below this level?

Solution: z = (120 − 85) / 15 = 35/15 = 2.33. From z-table: z = 2.33 corresponds to the 99.0th percentile. So ~99% of healthy people have creatinine below 120 μmol/L, and ~1% have values above this.

Clinical interpretation: A creatinine of 120 is well above normal (z > 1.96), suggesting possible renal impairment — but only 1% of healthy people would have this value, so specificity for disease depends on the clinical context.

Scenario: A study measures blood pressure in 64 patients. Mean SBP = 135 mmHg, SD = 20 mmHg. Calculate SEM and 95% CI for the mean.

Solution: SEM = 20/√64 = 20/8 = 2.5 mmHg. 95% CI = 135 ± 1.96 × 2.5 = 135 ± 4.9 = (130.1, 139.9) mmHg.

Interpretation: We are 95% confident the true population mean SBP lies between 130.1 and 139.9 mmHg. If the study had only 16 patients (SEM = 20/4 = 5 mmHg), the CI would be ±9.8 — almost twice as wide, giving a much less precise estimate.

Key insight: The sample size has a square-root relationship with precision. Quadrupling n halves the SEM and narrows the CI by half.

Scenario: Hospital stay durations follow a right-skewed distribution (median 4 days, range 1–60 days). You take 100 random samples of n = 40 each and calculate the mean of each sample. What shape will the distribution of these sample means have?

Solution: The distribution of sample means will be approximately normally distributed (by the CLT) with mean ≈ population mean (about 5.5 days given the skew) and SD ≈ population SD / √40.

Clinical importance: This is why we can use a t-test to compare mean hospital stays between two groups, even though the individual stay durations are highly skewed — as long as each group has at least 30 patients. The CLT ensures the sample means are approximately normal.

🧠 Active Recall Questions

  • Q: State the Central Limit Theorem in your own words.
    A: The sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as sample size increases, regardless of the shape of the underlying population distribution.
  • Q: What is the difference between SD and SEM?
    A: SD describes spread of individual observations around the mean. SEM = SD/√n describes how precisely the sample mean estimates the population mean. SEM is always ≤ SD.
  • Q: What z-value corresponds to a two-tailed 95% confidence interval?
    A: z = 1.96. This means 95% of values in a standard normal distribution lie between −1.96 and +1.96.
  • Q: How does sample size affect the SEM?
    A: SEM = σ/√n. Larger n gives smaller SEM. To halve the SEM, you need to quadruple n.
  • Q: What is a Q-Q plot and what does it assess?
    A: A Q-Q plot (quantile-quantile plot) compares sample quantiles to theoretical normal quantiles. Points should lie on the diagonal line if the data is normally distributed. Systematic deviations suggest non-normality.

📖 Glossary

Normal distribution
Symmetric, bell-shaped distribution defined by μ and σ².
Standard Normal (Z)
N(0, 1). Obtained by z = (x−μ)/σ.
z-score
Number of standard deviations a value is from the mean.
Central Limit Theorem
Sample mean ~ N(μ, σ²/n) as n→∞, for any underlying distribution.
Standard Error (SEM)
σ/√n. Precision of the sample mean estimate.
Sampling distribution
Distribution of a statistic (e.g., mean) across many samples.
Q-Q plot
Plot of sample quantiles vs theoretical normal quantiles. Tests normality.
Shapiro-Wilk test
Formal statistical test for normality. Best for n < 50.
Log transformation
y = ln(x). Used to normalise right-skewed data.
Geometric mean
Back-transformed mean of log-transformed data. ≤ arithmetic mean for skewed data.
