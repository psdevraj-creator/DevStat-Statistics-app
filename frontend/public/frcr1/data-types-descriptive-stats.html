Data Types & Descriptive Statistics

Measurement scales, data classification, central tendency, dispersion, skewness, and kurtosis — GCSE to FRCR level.

Types of Data

Data comes in different types, and the type determines what statistics you can calculate:

  • Categorical (Qualitative) — values are categories. Nominal: named categories with no order (e.g., blood group A, B, AB, O; alive/dead). Ordinal: ordered categories (e.g., tumour stage I, II, III, IV; pain score 0–10).
  • Numerical (Quantitative) — values are numbers. Discrete: whole numbers (e.g., number of lymph nodes, number of hospital visits). Continuous: any value within a range (e.g., height, weight, blood pressure, dose in Gy).
⚠️ Key Distinction
Always check: is the variable categorical or numerical? This is the first step in choosing the right statistical test. Ordinal data is often treated as categorical (using non-parametric tests) or as continuous depending on the analysis.

Figure 1: The measurement scale staircase. Each step adds more information — ratio data permits all arithmetic operations, while nominal data only permits counting.

Describing Data — Averages & Spread

Measures of central tendency (where the "middle" is):

  • Mean — the arithmetic average (sum ÷ n). Sensitive to outliers.
  • Median — the middle value when data is ordered. Not affected by outliers.
  • Mode — the most frequent value.

Measures of spread (how variable the data is):

  • Range — max − min. Simple but very sensitive to outliers.
  • Interquartile Range (IQR) — Q3 − Q1. Contains the middle 50% of data. Used with median.
  • Variance — average of squared deviations from the mean.
  • Standard Deviation (SD) — square root of variance. Describes spread of individual data points. Used with mean.

Rule of thumb: If data is normally distributed, use mean ± SD. If skewed, use median (IQR). Always check the distribution first!

Frequency Distributions & Box Plots

Data can be summarised using frequency tables, bar charts (for categorical data), histograms (for numerical data), and box plots (showing median, IQR, and outliers). A histogram shows the shape of the distribution: symmetric, skewed left, or skewed right.

Figure 2: Box-and-whisker plot anatomy. The box spans Q1 to Q3 (IQR). The central line is the median. Whiskers extend to min/max (or 1.5×IQR). Circles beyond whiskers are outliers.

Measurement Scales — The Full Classification

Correct classification of data type determines choice of statistical method. The FRCR exam frequently tests this.

Nominal data: Categories with no intrinsic order. Examples: blood group, sex, tumour histology (squamous, adenocarcinoma, small cell). Analysis: proportions, chi-square test, logistic regression.

Ordinal data: Ordered categories with unequal intervals. Examples: ECOG performance status (0–4), AJCC stage (I–IV), pain scale, Likert items. Analysis: median + IQR, Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal-Wallis, ordinal logistic regression.

Interval data: Numerical with equal intervals but no true zero. Example: temperature in °C. Can calculate mean, but ratios are meaningless (20°C is not "twice as hot" as 10°C).

Ratio data: Numerical with a true zero. Examples: height, weight, dose in Gy, tumour volume, creatinine level. All arithmetic operations possible. Most biological measurements are ratio data.

Discrete vs Continuous: Discrete data takes integer values (number of metastases, number of fractions, number of hospital admissions). Continuous data can take any value on a continuum (creatinine level, tumour diameter, PSA, time to event).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Data Type Misclassification
FRCR questions often present data and ask "Which test should be used?" The most common mistake is treating ordinal data as continuous (e.g., using mean ± SD for ECOG score) or treating continuous data that is non-normal as requiring a parametric test. Always check distribution first.

The Mean — Properties & Limitations

Population mean (μ): μ = Σxᵢ / N. Sample mean (x̄): x̄ = Σxᵢ / n.

The mean is the least squares estimator — it minimises the sum of squared deviations (Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²). This property makes it the foundation for ANOVA and regression.

Limitations:

  • The mean is highly sensitive to outliers. A single extreme value can shift the mean substantially.
  • For skewed distributions, the mean is pulled towards the tail and no longer represents the "typical" value.
  • For bimodal distributions, the mean may fall between the two peaks and represent no one.

Weighted mean: Used when observations have different importance. x̄_w = Σ(wᵢ × xᵢ) / Σwᵢ. Example: calculating overall exam score when different components have different weightings.

The Median — Properties & Use Cases

Median: The 50th percentile — the value below which 50% of observations fall. For n ordered values: if n is odd, median = value at position (n+1)/2. If n is even, median = average of values at positions n/2 and n/2+1.

Advantages: Robust to outliers — the median is unaffected by extreme values. Preferred for skewed distributions (survival times, economic data, tumour sizes, hospital stay durations).

Other percentiles: Q1 (25th percentile), Q3 (75th percentile). Together with the median, these form the five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum.

When to use mean vs median in FRCR: Survival times are nearly always reported as median survival (not mean), because survival data is right-skewed (a few patients live much longer, pulling the mean up). Tumour sizes should use mean ± SD if normally distributed, but median ± IQR if skewed.

Standard Deviation & Variance

Population variance (σ²): σ² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N.

Sample variance (s²): s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1). The denominator (n − 1) gives Bessel's correction for sample variance — it corrects the bias introduced because we estimated the mean from the sample.

Standard Deviation (SD): √(variance). SD is in the same units as the original data, making it directly interpretable.

Coefficient of Variation (CV): (SD / mean) × 100%. Allows comparison of variability between datasets with different units or means. Example: comparing variability of tumour size measurements between different imaging modalities (CT vs MRI), or comparing variability of PSA to variability of tumour diameter.

Properties of SD:

  • If data is normally distributed: ~68% within ±1 SD, ~95% within ±1.96 SD, ~99.7% within ±3 SD.
  • SD is always ≥ 0. SD = 0 means all values are identical.
  • SD is sensitive to outliers (because it uses squared deviations).

Skewness & Kurtosis

Skewness measures asymmetry of a distribution:

  • Positive skew (right tail): Mean > median. The tail extends to the right. Examples: survival times, PSA levels, hospital stay durations, radiation dose to organs at risk (many low values, few high values). Use median ± IQR.
  • Negative skew (left tail): Mean < median. The tail extends to the left. Examples: age at menopause, some quality-of-life scores, time to complete a task after training.
  • Zero skew: Symmetric. Mean = median = mode. Normally distributed data.

Kurtosis measures the "tailedness" of a distribution:

  • Leptokurtic (high kurtosis): Heavy tails, more outliers. Example: financial returns data.
  • Platykurtic (low kurtosis): Light tails, fewer outliers. Example: uniform distribution.
  • Mesokurtic: Normal distribution (kurtosis = 3).

Exam relevance: Skewness determines whether to use mean ± SD or median ± IQR. Kurtosis is less commonly tested but may appear in the context of outlier detection and model assumptions.

⚠️ Common Exam Traps
• ECOG performance status (0–4) is ORDINAL, not continuous — use median ± IQR, NOT mean ± SD.
• Tumour stage I–IV is ordinal, not interval — the difference between I and II may not equal II and III.
• "SD vs SEM" — if the question asks for variability of individual observations, answer SD, not SEM.
• Bessel's correction (n−1) applies to sample variance, not population variance (which uses N).
• The coefficient of variation is meaningless when the mean is near zero.
• For ordinal data, you CANNOT calculate a mean — only median and mode are appropriate.
🏥 Why This Matters Clinically

Reporting tumour response: When a clinical trial reports "mean tumour size reduction," check whether the data was normally distributed. If it was skewed (e.g., some patients had dramatic responses while most had modest ones), the mean may overstate the typical response. Median reduction is often more clinically representative.

Quality of life data: QoL scores (EORTC QLQ-C30, FACT-G) produce ordinal data from Likert scales. These should be summarised as median ± IQR, not mean ± SD, yet many published papers incorrectly use parametric summaries for ordinal QoL data.

ECOG performance status: This ordinal scale (0–5) is a powerful predictor of outcomes in oncology. It must be analysed using non-parametric methods or ordinal logistic regression. Treating ECOG as continuous (e.g., "mean ECOG = 1.3") is statistically incorrect.

Cost data in health economics: Healthcare costs are almost always right-skewed (most patients cost little, a few cost a lot). Summary statistics must use median ± IQR, and cost comparisons typically use non-parametric tests or logarithmic transformations.

When critically appraising a paper: Always check whether the authors used the correct summary measure for the data type. Incorrect use of mean ± SD for skewed data is one of the most common statistical errors in medical literature.

📐 Worked Examples

Scenario: A study records the following variables. Classify each by measurement scale:

a) Blood group (A, B, AB, O)
b) ECOG performance status (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
c) Temperature in °C
d) Tumour volume in cm³
e) Number of positive lymph nodes
f) Pain score (none = 0, mild = 1, moderate = 2, severe = 3)

Solution:

a) Nominal — categories with no natural order. Blood groups are named, not ranked.
b) Ordinal — ordered categories. The difference between ECOG 0 and 1 is not the same as between 1 and 2.
c) Interval — equal intervals but no true zero. 0°C does not mean "no temperature."
d) Ratio — true zero. A tumour of 0 cm³ means no tumour. Ratios meaningful (20 cm³ is twice 10 cm³).
e) Discrete numerical (ratio) — counts are whole numbers with a true zero.
f) Ordinal — ordered categories with unequal intervals. Do NOT treat as continuous.

Logic: Always ask: (1) Is it categorical or numerical? (2) If categorical, is there a natural order? (3) If numerical, does it have a true zero?

Scenario: Five patients have hospital stay durations (days): 2, 3, 4, 5, 20. Calculate mean and median. Which is more representative?

Solution: Mean = (2+3+4+5+20)/5 = 34/5 = 6.8 days. Median = 4 days (the 3rd ordered value).

The median (4 days) better represents the typical stay for most patients. The mean (6.8 days) is pulled up by the single outlier (20 days). Most patients stayed 2–5 days, so the median is more clinically representative.

Key insight: Mean ± SD should only be used for symmetric, approximately normally distributed data. For skewed data (like hospital stays), always report median ± IQR.

Scenario: Tumour diameters (cm): 2.1, 2.5, 2.8, 3.0, 3.6. Calculate SD, variance, and CV.

Solution:

Mean = (2.1+2.5+2.8+3.0+3.6)/5 = 14.0/5 = 2.8 cm.

Deviations: −0.7, −0.3, 0, 0.2, 0.8. Squared: 0.49, 0.09, 0, 0.04, 0.64. Sum = 1.26.

Sample variance (s²) = 1.26 / (5−1) = 1.26 / 4 = 0.315 cm².

Sample SD (s) = √0.315 = 0.561 cm (≈0.56 cm).

CV = (0.561 / 2.8) × 100% = 20.0%.

Interpretation: The SD of 0.56 cm means typical individual tumours vary by ~0.56 cm from the mean. The CV of 20% allows comparison with other tumour size datasets (e.g., if another study reports CV = 30%, that study's data is more variable relative to its mean).

🧠 Active Recall Questions

  • Q: Name the four levels of measurement and give an example of each.
    A: Nominal (blood group), Ordinal (ECOG performance status), Interval (temperature °C), Ratio (tumour volume cm³).
  • Q: What summary statistic should you use for skewed data?
    A: Median ± IQR (interquartile range). The mean is inappropriate because it is pulled towards the tail of the distribution.
  • Q: What is Bessel's correction and why is it used?
    A: Dividing by (n−1) instead of n when calculating sample variance. It corrects the bias introduced by estimating the population mean from the sample.
  • Q: What does the coefficient of variation tell you?
    A: CV = (SD/mean) × 100%. It allows comparison of variability between datasets with different units or means. Higher CV = more relative variability.
  • Q: Why is ECOG performance status considered ordinal and not continuous?
    A: The difference between ECOG 0 and 1 is not necessarily the same as between 1 and 2. The intervals are not equal, so it cannot be treated as continuous/interval data.
