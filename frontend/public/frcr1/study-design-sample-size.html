Study Design, Sample Size & Errors

RCTs, blinding, ITT vs PP analysis, non-inferiority, sample size calculations, multiple testing, confounding, Simpson's paradox, and bias — GCSE to FRCR level.

Types of Study Design

Studies are classified by whether the researcher intervenes:

  • Observational: Researcher watches without intervening. Includes cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies.
  • Experimental (interventional): Researcher allocates treatments. The gold standard is the Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT).

Hierarchy of evidence (strongest → weakest): Systematic reviews / meta-analyses → RCTs → cohort studies → case-control → case series → expert opinion.

Figure 1: Evidence hierarchy — systematic reviews provide the strongest evidence; expert opinion the weakest.

The RCT and Blinding

  • Randomisation: Balances known and unknown confounders.
  • Control group: Provides a baseline comparator.
  • Single-blind: Participants unaware of treatment.
  • Double-blind: Participants AND assessors unaware.
  • Triple-blind: Adds data analysts to the blinded parties.
  • Allocation concealment: Hides allocation from those enrolling patients — prevents selection bias before randomisation.

Types of Bias

  • Selection bias: Non-representative sample.
  • Recall bias: Cases remember exposures better than controls.
  • Performance bias: Different care given between groups (prevented by blinding).
  • Detection bias: Outcomes measured differently between groups.
  • Attrition bias: Dropouts differ systematically between groups.
  • Publication bias: Positive results more likely to be published.

Key point: Bias is a systematic error — unlike random error, it cannot be reduced by increasing sample size.

Randomisation Methods

Simple randomisation: Coin flip equivalent. Balanced groups for large n (>200) but may be unbalanced for small n.

Block randomisation: Ensures periodic balance (e.g., blocks of 4). Block size should be random and concealed.

Stratified randomisation: Randomise within strata (centre, stage, age). Essential for multicentre trials.

Minimisation: Adaptive method minimising imbalance across multiple factors. More efficient but not strictly random.

Cluster randomisation: Groups (clinics, hospitals) randomised together. Requires intraclass correlation (ICC) adjustment. ICC reflects how similar patients within a cluster are.

Intention-to-Treat vs Per-Protocol Analysis

Intention-to-treat (ITT): All randomised patients analysed in their allocated group, regardless of compliance. Preserves randomisation — the gold standard for superiority trials. Conservative estimate of efficacy.

Per-protocol (PP): Only patients who completed treatment as planned. May overestimate efficacy (excludes non-compliers who may have worse outcomes). Primary analysis for non-inferiority trials — because ITT dilutes the active comparison.

Modified ITT (mITT): Excludes patients with no post-baseline data. Pragmatic compromise used in oncology.

⚠️ Exam Trap
ITT = primary for superiority. PP = primary for non-inferiority. This reversal is a frequent FRCR exam question.

Non-Inferiority & Equivalence Trials

Non-inferiority margin (δ): Pre-specified maximum acceptable inferiority. If the upper bound of the 95% CI for (new − control) does not exceed δ, non-inferiority is demonstrated.

Equivalence: Requires BOTH bounds of the 95% CI to fall within ±δ.

Figure 2: CI interpretation for non-inferiority trials. NI is demonstrated only when the upper CI bound stays within the pre-specified margin δ.

Sample Size Calculation

Requires: effect size (δ), significance level (α), power (1−β), and variability (σ).

n per group = 2σ²(z_α/2 + z_β)² / δ²

z_α/2 = 1.96 (α = 0.05, two-tailed); z_β = 0.84 (80% power) or 1.28 (90% power).

  • Halve δ → 4× the sample size (n ∝ 1/δ²).
  • 80% → 90% power → ~33% more patients.
  • α 0.05 → 0.01 → ~20% more patients.

Multiple Testing

Running m tests at α = 0.05 gives family-wise error rate (FWER) = 1 − (1−0.05)^m. With 20 tests, expected ~1 false positive by chance.

Bonferroni: α* = α/m. Most conservative. Appropriate for small m and independent tests.

Holm-Bonferroni: Sequential, less conservative than Bonferroni.

False Discovery Rate (FDR — Benjamini-Hochberg): Controls expected proportion of false positives among rejected hypotheses. Preferred for large-scale testing (genomics). Less conservative.

⚠️ Subgroup Analyses
Unplanned subgroup analyses in a trial with 20 subgroups are unreliable — expect ~1 false positive at α = 0.05. Pre-specified subgroups are more credible.

Confounding & Simpson's Paradox

A confounder is associated with both the exposure and outcome, and is not in the causal pathway. Methods of control: randomisation (best), restriction, matching, stratification, multivariable regression, propensity score.

Simpson's Paradox: An association observed in aggregated data is reversed or eliminated when data is stratified by a confounder.

Figure 3: Simpson's Paradox. Treatment A is better in both strata, but appears worse overall because sicker patients (lower baseline survival) were more often given A.

Essential Exam Facts

Evidence hierarchy: SR/meta-analysis → RCT → cohort → case-control → cross-sectional → expert opinion.
ITT: all randomised patients in their allocated group. Primary for superiority trials.
PP: compliant patients only. Primary for NON-INFERIORITY trials.
NI margin δ: upper 95% CI must not exceed δ to demonstrate non-inferiority.
Sample size ∝ σ²/δ². Halving effect size → 4× the patients needed.
Double-blind: participants AND assessors unaware of allocation.
Allocation concealment ≠ blinding. Concealment is BEFORE; blinding is AFTER randomisation.
Bonferroni: α* = α/m. Most conservative. FDR: less conservative, preferred for large-scale testing.
FWER = 1 − (1−α)^m. With 20 tests at α=0.05, expect ~1 false positive.

⚠️ Common Exam Traps
• ITT for superiority; PP for non-inferiority — this reversal is a classic exam question.
• NI: check whether the CI upper bound exceeds δ, not just whether the point estimate is near zero.
• Bonferroni correction: multiply observed p by m and compare to α (equivalent to dividing α by m).
• p = 0.04 from 20 unplanned subgroups is NOT significant after multiple testing correction.
• Allocation concealment prevents selection bias BEFORE randomisation. Blinding prevents ascertainment/performance bias AFTER randomisation.
🏥 Why This Matters Clinically

CONSORT checklist for appraising RCTs: (1) Was randomisation truly random with allocation concealment? (2) Was blinding successful? (3) Was ITT analysis used? (4) Was the sample size calculated a priori? (5) Were subgroup analyses pre-specified? These are standard FRCR critical appraisal points.

Non-inferiority in oncology: Many modern trials test whether a less toxic treatment (fewer fractions, oral vs IV) is non-inferior to standard. Understanding the NI margin and CI interpretation is essential for appraising these trials.

Confounding by indication: In retrospective oncology studies, sicker patients often receive more aggressive treatment. A naive comparison showing worse outcomes with aggressive treatment may reflect confounding by indication, not a real harmful effect. Always look for adjusted analyses.

Multiple testing in genomics: GWAS studies test millions of SNPs. Genome-wide significance is usually p < 5×10⁻⁸ (Bonferroni for ~1 million tests). FDR is used for transcriptomic studies.

Publication bias in meta-analysis: Funnel plots (log effect size vs precision) should be symmetrical if there is no publication bias. Asymmetry suggests smaller studies with negative results were not published, inflating the pooled effect estimate.

📐 Worked Examples

Scenario: RCT comparing mean PSA. Expected difference δ = 2.0 ng/mL, SD σ = 5.0 ng/mL, α = 0.05 (two-tailed), 80% power. How many patients per group?

Solution: z_α/2 = 1.96, z_β = 0.84.

n = 2 × 25 × (1.96 + 0.84)² / 4 = 50 × 7.84 / 4 = 98 per group.

If δ halved to 1.0: n = 50 × 7.84 / 1 = 392 — 4× larger! If power increased to 90% (z_β = 1.28): n = 50 × (1.96+1.28)² / 4 = 50 × 10.50 / 4 ≈ 131 per group (+33%).

Scenario: 10 subgroup analyses. Three significant p-values: p = 0.04, 0.02, 0.001. Apply Bonferroni correction (α* = 0.05/10 = 0.005).

Solution: Only p = 0.001 < 0.005 survives. The p = 0.04 and p = 0.02 results are not significant after correction — likely false positives. This illustrates why post-hoc subgroup analyses must be interpreted cautiously.

Scenario: Superiority RCT, 200 per group. 40 in treatment group stopped early. ITT: 5-year survival 62% vs 55% (p = 0.04). PP: 72% vs 55% (p = 0.001). Which is primary and why?

Solution: ITT is primary for a superiority trial. PP overestimates efficacy by excluding non-compliers (who likely had worse outcomes). ITT gives an unbiased, real-world estimate of effectiveness. If this were a non-inferiority trial, PP would be primary — excluding non-compliers is appropriate because they blur the distinction between treatments.

🧠 Active Recall Questions

  • Q: What is the difference between allocation concealment and blinding?
    A: Allocation concealment prevents selection bias BEFORE randomisation (hides the sequence from enrolment staff). Blinding prevents performance/detection bias AFTER randomisation (hides allocation from participants and assessors).
  • Q: For which trial type is per-protocol analysis the primary analysis?
    A: Non-inferiority trials. ITT is primary for superiority. In NI trials, ITT biases toward showing NI by including non-compliers who narrow the difference between groups.
  • Q: Halving the effect size requires how much larger a sample?
    A: 4× larger. Sample size ∝ 1/δ². Halving δ means the denominator decreases by 4-fold, so n must increase 4-fold.
  • Q: What is Simpson's Paradox?
    A: An association in aggregated data reverses or disappears when a confounding variable is accounted for by stratification.
  • Q: When is FDR preferred over Bonferroni correction?
    A: In large-scale testing (genomics, transcriptomics) where Bonferroni is too conservative and would miss many true discoveries. FDR controls the expected proportion of false positives among rejections, not the probability of any false positive.

📖 Glossary

RCT
Randomised controlled trial. Gold standard. Random allocation to treatment/control.
Allocation concealment
Hiding allocation sequence from enrollers. Prevents selection bias before randomisation.
Double-blind
Participants AND outcome assessors unaware of group allocation.
ITT analysis
Analyse in allocated group regardless of compliance. Primary for superiority.
Per-protocol
Only compliant patients analysed. Primary for non-inferiority trials.
NI margin (δ)
Pre-specified acceptable inferiority threshold. Upper CI must not exceed δ.
Bonferroni
α* = α/m. Most conservative multiple testing correction.
FDR (BH)
False discovery rate. Controls expected proportion of false positives. Less conservative than Bonferroni.
Confounding
Variable associated with both exposure and outcome, not in causal pathway.
Simpson's Paradox
Aggregated association reversed after stratification by confounder.
Publication bias
Positive studies preferentially published. Funnel plot asymmetry detects it.
ICC
Intraclass correlation. Within-cluster similarity in cluster-randomised trials.
