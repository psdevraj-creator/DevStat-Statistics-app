Survival Analysis

Kaplan-Meier curves, log-rank test, Cox proportional hazards model, hazard ratios, censoring, competing risks, and assumptions — GCSE to FRCR level.

Survival Data — The Basics

Survival analysis deals with "time-to-event" data — how long until a patient dies, has a recurrence, or some other event. The key feature is censoring: some patients haven't had the event by the end of the study, or they drop out. We still use their information up to the point they left the study.

Censoring: A patient is "censored" if we don't know their exact event time. Common reasons: (1) Patient is still alive at the end of follow-up, (2) Patient is lost to follow-up, (3) Patient dies from a different cause. Censored patients are represented as tick marks on Kaplan-Meier curves.

Kaplan-Meier curve: A step-function graph showing the proportion of patients surviving over time. It accounts for censoring by adjusting the denominator at each event time.

Median survival: The time at which 50% of patients have experienced the event (or are censored before that point). This is the most commonly reported survival summary in oncology.

Figure 1: Kaplan-Meier survival curves comparing treatment vs control groups. Each step down represents one or more events. Tick marks (|) are censored patients. Median survival is read at the 50% line. Numbers at risk decrease as patients have events or are censored.

Kaplan-Meier Estimator — Full Detail

Product-limit estimator (Kaplan-Meier): Ŝ(t) = Π (nᵢ − dᵢ) / nᵢ, where the product is over all event times ≤ t, nᵢ = number at risk just before time tᵢ, and dᵢ = number of events at time tᵢ.

Properties:

  • Only defined at event times — between events, survival is constant (step function).
  • Each step down represents an event at that time.
  • Censored patients contribute to the "number at risk" until they are censored, after which they are removed from the denominator.
  • The curve becomes less reliable at later times (fewer patients at risk).

Confidence intervals around KM estimates: Greenwood's formula calculates SE(Ŝ(t)), used to construct CIs. CIs widen over time as fewer patients remain at risk.

Median survival from KM curve: The time Ŝ(t) = 0.50. If the curve never drops below 0.50, median survival cannot be calculated — report survival at 1, 2, or 5 years instead.

Survival at specific time points: Often reported as "1-year survival," "5-year survival," etc. These are vertical slices through the KM curve at specific times.

Log-Rank Test

Use: Compares survival distributions between two (or more) groups. Tests H₀: the survival curves are the same across groups.

How it works: At each event time, calculates expected number of events in each group assuming H₀ (proportional to number at risk). Sums observed − expected across all times. χ²-like statistic with (k−1) df.

Assumptions:

  1. Survival curves are comparable (non-crossing) — the ratio of hazards is constant over time (proportional hazards).
  2. Censoring is independent of the event (non-informative censoring).
  3. Groups are comparable at baseline (randomisation helps ensure this).

Limitation: The log-rank test only tells you whether the curves differ overall — it does not estimate the magnitude of the difference. For that, you need Cox regression (hazard ratio).

Weighted log-rank tests: The standard log-rank test is most powerful when the proportional hazards assumption holds. Alternatives (Peto-Peto, Gehan-Wilcoxon) give more weight to early events and may be more powerful when hazards are not proportional.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Log-Rank Assumptions
The log-rank test assumes proportional hazards (the ratio of event rates between groups is constant over time). If survival curves cross, the log-rank test is not appropriate — use alternative tests or time-dependent covariates in Cox regression.

Cox Proportional Hazards Model

Semi-parametric model: h(t|x) = h₀(t) × exp(β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ... + βₖxₖ).

h(t|x) = hazard at time t for a patient with covariates x. h₀(t) = baseline hazard (unspecified — not estimated). exp(βᵢ) = Hazard Ratio (HR) for predictor xᵢ.

Hazard Ratio interpretation:

  • HR = 1: no effect on hazard.
  • HR > 1: increased hazard (worse survival).
  • HR < 1: decreased hazard (better survival).

Example: HR = 0.65 for treatment (vs control) means the treatment reduces the hazard of death by 35% at any given time, assuming proportional hazards.

Why semi-parametric? The baseline hazard h₀(t) is left unspecified — the model makes no assumptions about its shape. Only the regression coefficients (β) are estimated parametrically. This is the key advantage of Cox regression — it is robust to the shape of the survival curve.

Partial likelihood estimation: Cox models use a "partial likelihood" that considers only the order of events, not the exact times. This is mathematically elegant and computationally efficient.

Proportional Hazards Assumption — Checking & Violations

Checking the assumption:

  • Log-log survival plot: Plot log(−log(Ŝ(t))) vs log(t) for each group. Parallel lines indicate proportional hazards. Crossing lines indicate violation.
  • Schoenfeld residuals: Test correlation between residuals and time. A significant test (p < 0.05) indicates non-proportionality.
  • Time-dependent covariates: Include an interaction term between the predictor and time. If significant, the hazard ratio changes over time — proportional hazards assumption is violated.

What to do if violated:

  • Stratified Cox model: Stratify by the offending variable (allow different baseline hazards for different levels of the variable) while estimating other coefficients.
  • Time-dependent covariates: Include a time-by-predictor interaction to model time-varying HR.
  • Parametric survival models: Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic — these assume specific hazard shapes but can handle non-proportional hazards.
  • Landmark analysis: Fix a time point and analyse survival from that point forward.

Censoring — Types & Assumptions

Right censoring (most common): Patient is event-free at last follow-up. Common reasons: study ends, patient withdraws, patient dies of other cause.

Left censoring: The event happened before the study started (e.g., a test that was positive "sometime in the past"). Less common.

Interval censoring: The event occurred between two observation times (e.g., between two clinic visits). Common in oncology (tumour progression detected at imaging visits).

Non-informative censoring assumption: Censored patients should have the same future risk of the event as patients who remain in the study (conditional on covariates). This is violated if patients drop out because they are too sick (informative censoring). For example, if very ill patients drop out and are censored, the remaining patients appear to have better survival than they truly do.

Competing risks: When patients can experience one of several mutually exclusive events (e.g., cancer death vs cardiovascular death). Standard KM analysis treats competing events as censoring — this can bias estimates. Use Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF) with Gray's test instead of KM/log-rank. The Fine-Gray model is a regression model for competing risks data.

Key Differences: Cox vs Logistic Regression

Feature Logistic Regression Cox Regression
OutcomeBinary (event/no event at fixed time)Time to event (handles censoring)
Effect measureOdds Ratio (OR)Hazard Ratio (HR)
EstimationMLE (full likelihood)Partial likelihood
Key assumptionLinear log-oddsProportional hazards
Time componentIgnores time (binary at fixed point)Explicitly models time

When to use which: If all patients have complete follow-up to a fixed time point (e.g., 5-year survival yes/no), logistic regression can be used. However, Cox regression is preferred because it uses time information more efficiently and handles censoring naturally. Cox regression is the standard for time-to-event analysis in oncology.

Essential Exam Facts

KM curve: product-limit estimator of survival. Step function at event times.
Censoring: patient event-free at last follow-up. Tick marks on KM curve.
Median survival: time when Ŝ(t) = 0.50. Cannot be calculated if curve stays above 0.50.
Log-rank test: compares survival curves between groups. PH assumption.
Cox PH model: h(t) = h₀(t) × exp(βX). Semi-parametric. HR = exp(β).
Proportional hazards: HR is constant over time. Check with Schoenfeld residuals.
If PH violated: stratified Cox, time-dependent covariate, or parametric models.
Competing risks: use CIF + Fine-Gray model instead of KM + Cox.
Non-informative censoring: censored patients have same risk as those remaining.
Cox preferred over logistic regression for time-to-event data.
⚠️ Common Exam Traps
• HR is NOT relative risk — it is a ratio of HAZARDS (instantaneous event rates). HR and RR are different measures.
• Crossing KM curves → PH violated → do not use standard Cox or log-rank.
• If median survival is not reached, report survival at specific time points (e.g., 2-year OS = 72%).
• Competing risks must be handled differently — treating competing events as censoring overestimates the risk of the event of interest.
• Cox regression does NOT assume the shape of the baseline hazard — only that hazards are proportional.
🏥 Why This Matters Clinically

Oncology trials: Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) are the most common endpoints in cancer clinical trials. They are analysed using KM curves, log-rank tests for primary comparison, and Cox regression for adjusted HR with 95% CI.

Interpreting hazard ratios: An HR of 0.75 does NOT mean "25% of patients lived longer." It means the INSTANTANEOUS RISK of death is 25% lower at any given time. The absolute survival benefit depends on the baseline hazard (how aggressive the cancer is).

Landmark analysis for late effects: In radiotherapy trials, late toxicities may not appear for years. Landmark analysis (e.g., "survival without late toxicity at 5 years") is commonly used. KM curves with competing risk methods are essential for accurate estimates of toxicity rates.

Critical appraisal: When reading a survival analysis paper: (1) Are the KM curves clearly labelled with numbers at risk? (2) Was the log-rank test used appropriately (PH assumption checked)? (3) Are HRs reported with 95% CIs? (4) Was the PH assumption tested (Schoenfeld residuals or log-log plot)? (5) Were competing risks considered? (6) Is censoring explained and non-informative?

📐 Worked Examples

Scenario: A KM curve shows: median OS for treatment = 24 months (95% CI: 20–28), median OS for control = 18 months (95% CI: 15–21). 2-year survival: treatment 52%, control 38%. Log-rank p = 0.01. Interpret.

Solution: Median OS is 6 months longer with treatment (24 vs 18 months). At 2 years, 52% of treatment patients are alive vs 38% of controls — an absolute difference of 14 percentage points. The log-rank test is significant (p = 0.01), indicating the curves differ overall. The shaded areas (CIs) around each curve show the precision of the estimates — they should not overlap for statistical significance at individual time points.

Key insight: Always check the number at risk table below KM curves. If few patients remain at 5 years, the 5-year survival estimate is unreliable.

Scenario: A Cox model for OS includes: treatment (new vs standard, HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.85), age (per 10 years, HR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.10–1.31), ECOG (per 1 point, HR = 1.45, 95% CI 1.30–1.62). Interpret.

Solution: Adjusted for age and ECOG, the new treatment reduces the hazard of death by 35% (HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.85). This is statistically significant (CI does not include 1.0).

Each 10-year increase in age increases hazard by 20% (HR = 1.20), adjusted for treatment and ECOG.

Each 1-point increase in ECOG increases hazard by 45% (HR = 1.45), adjusted for treatment and age — ECOG is the strongest independent predictor.

Key insight: The natural log of HR is the coefficient (β). So β_treatment = ln(0.65) = −0.43. The negative value indicates reduced hazard.

Scenario: You run a Cox model and the Schoenfeld residual test gives p = 0.003 for the treatment variable. The log-log survival curves for the two treatment groups show converging lines. What does this mean and what can you do?

Solution: The proportional hazards assumption is violated (p = 0.003 < 0.05; non-parallel log-log lines). The treatment effect changes over time — the survival curves converge (hazard ratio is not constant).

Options: (1) Include a time-by-treatment interaction term. (2) Use a stratified Cox model by treatment. (3) Report a time-varying HR (e.g., HR in first year = 0.50, HR after first year = 0.85). (4) Use parametric survival models.

Clinical relevance: A treatment might have a strong early effect but wane over time. Reporting a single HR would be misleading — it would underestimate the early effect or overestimate the late effect.

🧠 Active Recall Questions

  • Q: What is censoring in survival analysis?
    A: A patient is censored if their exact event time is not known — they were event-free at last follow-up, lost to follow-up, or died from a competing cause. Their data contributes to the analysis up to the point of censoring.
  • Q: What does the log-rank test compare?
    A: It compares the overall survival distributions between groups. H₀: the survival curves are identical. It assumes proportional hazards (non-crossing curves).
  • Q: What is the proportional hazards assumption in Cox regression?
    A: The hazard ratio between groups is constant over time. Checked by Schoenfeld residuals (p > 0.05 = OK) or log-log survival plots (parallel lines = OK).
  • Q: How does Cox regression differ from logistic regression for time-to-event data?
    A: Cox regression uses time-to-event data and handles censoring, giving a hazard ratio. Logistic regression ignores time and censoring, giving an OR at a fixed time point. Cox is the standard for survival data.
  • Q: What are competing risks and how should they be analysed?
    A: Competing risks occur when patients can experience one of several mutually exclusive events (e.g., cancer death vs cardiac death). Use Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF) and Fine-Gray model instead of KM and Cox.

📖 Glossary

Kaplan-Meier
Non-parametric estimator of survival. Product-limit method.
Censoring
Patient event-free at last follow-up. Right censoring is most common.
Median survival
Time when 50% of patients have had the event (or are censored).
Log-rank test
Compares survival curves between groups. Assumes proportional hazards.
Cox PH model
Semi-parametric regression for survival. h(t) = h₀(t) × exp(βX).
Hazard Ratio (HR)
exp(β) from Cox model. Ratio of instantaneous event rates.
Proportional hazards
Assumption that HR is constant over time.
Schoenfeld residuals
Used to test proportional hazards assumption. p < 0.05 = violation.
Partial likelihood
Estimation method for Cox models. Based on event ordering.
Competing risks
Multiple mutually exclusive events. Use CIF + Fine-Gray model.
Fine-Gray model
Regression model for competing risks. Subdistribution hazard ratio.
Landmark analysis
Analysis from a fixed time point. For later outcomes.
