Parametric Tests — t-tests & ANOVA

One-sample, independent and paired t-tests; one-way and two-way ANOVA; assumptions, post-hoc tests, and interpretation — GCSE to FRCR level.

Comparing Groups — The Basic Idea

In medical research, we often want to compare groups. Did the treatment group do better than the control group? Is the new drug more effective than the standard treatment?

t-test: Used to compare the means of two groups. For example, comparing blood pressure between a drug group and a placebo group.

Paired t-test: Used when the same subjects are measured twice (before and after treatment). Each subject acts as their own control.

ANOVA: Used to compare means of three or more groups. For example, comparing tumour shrinkage across three treatment regimens.

When to Use Which Test

  • Two groups, independent (e.g., drug vs placebo, different patients) → Independent t-test.
  • Two groups, paired (e.g., before vs after in same patients) → Paired t-test.
  • Three+ groups (e.g., low dose, high dose, placebo) → ANOVA.

All these tests assume the data is approximately normally distributed and the groups have similar variability.

One-sample t-test

Use: Compares a sample mean to a known population mean (or hypothesised value).

Formula: t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n), where μ₀ is the hypothesised population mean.

Degrees of freedom: df = n − 1.

Example: Comparing mean tumour volume in a cohort of 25 patients to a known historical mean of 50 cm³. If x̄ = 45 cm³ and s = 15 cm³, t = (45−50) / (15/√25) = −5/3 = −1.67. For a two-tailed test with df=24, critical t at α=0.05 is ±2.064 — not significant (|t| < critical value).

Assumptions: Data approximately normally distributed (or n ≥ 30). Independence of observations.

Independent (Two-sample) t-test

Use: Compares means of two independent groups.

Formula: t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / SE(x̄₁ − x̄₂).

Two forms:

  • Equal variances assumed (Student's t): Uses pooled variance estimate. s²_pooled = [(n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²] / (n₁ + n₂ − 2). Requires Levene's test to confirm equal variances.
  • Unequal variances (Welch's t): More robust. Does not assume equal variances. Adjusted degrees of freedom (Satterthwaite approximation). Preferred in modern practice — most statistical software defaults to Welch's t.

Assumptions:

  1. Independence of observations (both within and between groups).
  2. Normality of data in each group (or n ≥ 30 per group — CLT applies to each mean).
  3. Equal variances (if using Student's t — check with Levene's test).
⚠️ Exam Trap: Equal Variance Assumption
If Levene's test is significant (p < 0.05), variances are unequal. Use Welch's t-test, NOT Student's t. Many FRCR questions test whether you know to check this assumption before choosing the test version.

Paired t-test

Use: Compares means of related observations — before/after measurements, matched pairs, or crossover designs.

Formula: t = d̄ / (sd / √n), where d̄ = mean of the paired differences, sd = SD of the differences, n = number of pairs.

Degrees of freedom: df = n − 1 (where n = number of pairs).

Key advantage: More powerful than independent t-test because each subject acts as their own control, reducing between-subject variability. The paired t-test effectively removes the "noise" of individual differences.

Assumptions: Differences (not the original values) should be approximately normally distributed. Independence between pairs (but not within pairs — they are matched by design).

One-way ANOVA

Use: Compares means of ≥ 3 independent groups. Example: comparing mean pain scores across three treatment groups (drug A, drug B, placebo).

Logic: Total variation in the data is partitioned into:

  • Between-group variation (SSB): Variation explained by group differences.
  • Within-group variation (SSW): Residual/error variation unexplained by group membership.

F-statistic: F = MSB / MSW = (SSB / dfB) / (SSW / dfW), where dfB = k−1 and dfW = N−k (k = number of groups, N = total observations).

If group means differ substantially, F is large and the p-value is small.

Figure 2: ANOVA partitions total variation (SST) into between-group variation (SSB, explained by group membership) and within-group variation (SSW, residual error). A large F-statistic means between-group variation dominates.

Assumptions:

  1. Independence of observations.
  2. Normality of residuals (not the original data, but the residuals).
  3. Homogeneity of variances across groups (Levene's test).

Post-hoc tests: If ANOVA is significant, it tells you at least one group differs — but not which ones. Post-hoc tests identify specific group differences while controlling for multiple comparisons:

  • Bonferroni: Most conservative (highest Type II error). Adjusts α by number of comparisons: α/m.
  • Tukey HSD: Controls family-wise error rate for all pairwise comparisons. Less conservative than Bonferroni when comparing all pairs.
  • Scheffé: Most conservative, for complex contrasts (not just pairwise comparisons).
  • Dunnett: Compares all groups to a single control group — more powerful than Bonferroni for this specific situation.

Two-way ANOVA

Use: Tests effects of two categorical factors and their interaction on a continuous outcome.

Example: Effect of treatment (drug vs placebo) AND sex (male vs female) on blood pressure reduction. The interaction term tests whether the treatment effect differs between males and females.

Sources of variation: Factor A, Factor B, A×B interaction, and residual error.

Interpretation:

  • Main effect of A: Is there an overall difference between levels of A, averaging over B?
  • Main effect of B: Is there an overall difference between levels of B, averaging over A?
  • Interaction (A×B): Does the effect of A depend on the level of B? If significant, main effects are difficult to interpret independently — report subgroup effects separately.

Repeated measures ANOVA: Extension of paired t-test to ≥ 3 time points or conditions. Accounts for correlation between repeated observations on the same subject. Assumption of sphericity (equal variances of differences between all pairs of time points) — checked by Mauchly's test. If violated, use Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldt correction.

What to Do When ANOVA Assumptions Are Violated

  • Non-normal residuals: Transform the data (log, square root) or use non-parametric alternative (Kruskal-Wallis test).
  • Unequal variances: Use Welch's ANOVA (does not assume equal variances) or transform the data.
  • Non-independence: Use mixed models (random effects) or repeated measures ANOVA.

Kruskal-Wallis test: Non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA. Based on ranks. Does not assume normality or equal variances. Less powerful when assumptions are met but more robust when they are violated.

Essential Exam Facts

One-sample t-test: compares sample mean to hypothesised population mean. df = n−1.
Independent t-test: two groups. Use Welch's t if variances unequal (Levene's test).
Paired t-test: before/after or matched pairs. More powerful than independent t-test.
Paired t-test analyses DIFFERENCES: t = d̄ / (sd/√n).
One-way ANOVA: ≥3 groups. F = MSB/MSW. Post-hoc: Tukey, Bonferroni, Dunnett.
Two-way ANOVA: two factors + interaction. Significant interaction → report subgroups.
ANOVA assumptions: independence, normality of residuals, equal variances.
Kruskal-Wallis: non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA.
Repeated measures ANOVA: ≥3 time points. Assumes sphericity (Mauchly's test).
Post-hoc tests control for multiple comparisons after significant ANOVA.
⚠️ Common Exam Traps
• Paired vs independent: if the data is paired (matched/before-after), use a PAIRED test. Using an independent test loses power and is statistically incorrect.
• ANOVA does NOT tell you which groups differ — only that at least one differs from the rest. You need post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons.
• Equal variances (Levene's test p > 0.05) are required for Student's t and standard ANOVA. If violated, use Welch's version.
• A significant interaction in two-way ANOVA means "the effect of factor A depends on the level of factor B." Do not interpret main effects in isolation.
• Repeated measures ANOVA assumes sphericity — check Mauchly's test before reporting results.
🏥 Why This Matters Clinically

RCT analysis: The independent t-test is the standard method for analysing continuous endpoints (blood pressure, tumour size, QoL scores) in two-arm randomised trials. The paired t-test is used for within-patient comparisons (crossover trials, before-after studies).

Dose-finding studies: ANOVA with post-hoc tests is used in phase II dose-ranging studies to identify which dose level(s) differ significantly from placebo and from each other. Dunnett's test is particularly useful for comparing multiple doses to a single control.

Subgroup analysis by centre: Two-way ANOVA with treatment-by-centre interaction tests whether treatment effects are consistent across centres in multicentre trials. A significant interaction would suggest the treatment works differently in different centres — important for generalisability.

Critical appraisal: When reviewing a paper: (1) Did they check the normality assumption? (2) Did they use the correct version of the t-test (equal vs unequal variances)? (3) Did they do post-hoc tests after ANOVA? (4) If repeated measures were used, was sphericity checked? Common errors in published papers include using independent t-test for paired data and omitting post-hoc tests after ANOVA.

📐 Worked Examples

Scenario: A crossover trial gives 20 patients both treatment A and treatment B (with washout period). Mean difference in pain score (A − B) = 2.1, SD of differences = 3.5. What test should be used? Calculate the test statistic.

Solution: Paired t-test (each patient receives both treatments — observations are paired).

t = d̄ / (sd / √n) = 2.1 / (3.5 / √20) = 2.1 / 0.783 = 2.68. df = 19.

Critical t(19) for α = 0.05 (two-tailed) = 2.093. Since 2.68 > 2.093, p < 0.05.

Conclusion: Statistically significant difference in pain scores between treatments A and B.

Key insight: Using an independent t-test here would be incorrect because it treats the data as 40 independent observations, ignoring the pairing. This inflates the error term (because between-subject variability is not removed) and would likely produce a non-significant result.

Scenario: A study compares tumour shrinkage (%, continuous) across three treatments: RT alone (n=15), chemo-RT (n=15), and chemo-RT + immunotherapy (n=15). ANOVA gives F = 5.42, p = 0.008. What do you conclude?

Solution: p = 0.008 < 0.05 → at least one group differs significantly from the others. ANOVA does NOT tell which groups differ — you need post-hoc tests.

Using Tukey HSD: compare all three pairs (RT vs chemo-RT, RT vs combo, chemo-RT vs combo). The significant pair(s) will have adjusted p-values < 0.05. The Tukey test controls the family-wise error rate for all pairwise comparisons.

Clinical importance: Without post-hoc tests, you cannot say whether the combination is better than chemo-RT alone, or whether both are better than RT. The ANOVA only tells you the overall model is significant.

Scenario: Two groups: group 1 (n=12, mean 8.2, SD 2.1), group 2 (n=14, mean 10.5, SD 4.3). Levene's test for equal variances gives p = 0.03. Which t-test version should you use?

Solution: Since Levene's test is significant (p = 0.03 < 0.05), variances are unequal (SD 2.1² vs 4.3²). Use Welch's t-test (unequal variances), NOT Student's t. Welch's t adjusts the degrees of freedom downward (Satterthwaite approximation) to account for the variance inequality.

Key insight: Many published papers incorrectly use Student's t without checking the equal variance assumption. Welch's t is robust and should be the default in modern practice.

🧠 Active Recall Questions

  • Q: What is the difference between Student's t and Welch's t?
    A: Student's t assumes equal variances between groups. Welch's t does not assume equal variances and adjusts df accordingly. Welch's is preferred in modern practice.
  • Q: Why is a paired t-test more powerful than an independent t-test?
    A: Each subject acts as their own control, removing between-subject variability from the error term. This reduces the SE and increases the chance of detecting a true difference.
  • Q: What does a significant ANOVA tell you, and what does it not tell you?
    A: It tells you at least one group mean differs from the others. It does NOT tell you which groups differ — you need post-hoc tests (Tukey, Bonferroni, Dunnett).
  • Q: What is an interaction in two-way ANOVA?
    A: The effect of one factor depends on the level of the other factor. If significant, do not interpret main effects separately — report subgroup-specific effects.
  • Q: What is sphericity in repeated measures ANOVA?
    A: The assumption that variances of the differences between all pairs of time points are equal. Checked by Mauchly's test. Corrected by Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldt if violated.

📖 Glossary

One-sample t-test
Compares sample mean to hypothesised population mean. df = n−1.
Independent t-test
Compares means of two independent groups. Student's (equal var) or Welch's (unequal).
Paired t-test
Compares means of paired observations. More powerful than independent t-test.
Levene's test
Tests equality of variances between groups. If p < 0.05, use Welch's t.
One-way ANOVA
Compares means of ≥3 groups. F = MSB/MSW.
Two-way ANOVA
Tests two factors and their interaction.
Post-hoc test
Follows significant ANOVA. Identifies which groups differ. Controls for multiple comparisons.
Tukey HSD
Post-hoc test for all pairwise comparisons. Controls family-wise error rate.
Dunnett's test
Post-hoc test comparing all groups to a single control.
Repeated measures ANOVA
For ≥3 time points / conditions on same subjects. Assumes sphericity.
Mauchly's test
Tests sphericity assumption in repeated measures ANOVA.
Kruskal-Wallis
Non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA. Rank-based.
